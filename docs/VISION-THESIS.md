# The Last Cycle: A Vision Thesis

## 1. The claim

SaaS has evolved through roughly five-year cycles, each defined by a new way of reaching users. We are now entering the last of these cycles — not the death of software, but the end of the current *form* of it. The window of momentum is a few years, not decades. Our bet: a company built deliberately for that window — native to it, profitable within it — can do very well, because windows reward the prepared more than moats reward the fortified.

## 2. Where SaaS has been

| Cycle | Shift | What changed |
|---|---|---|
| ~1999–2004 | **Delivery** | Software runs on someone else's servers (Salesforce) |
| ~2005–2009 | **Maturation** | Subscription economics cross the enterprise chasm |
| ~2010–2015 | **Distribution** | Mobile + self-serve; the buyer becomes the user (PLG) |
| ~2015–2020 | **Composition** | API-first; SaaS unbundles into composable parts (Stripe, Twilio) |
| ~2020–2025 | **Intelligence** | Consolidation platforms, then bolted-on AI copilots |

Two observations. First, every cycle killed a class of incumbent — never for lack of customers, but because their architecture assumed the previous runtime. Second, and critically: every prior cycle changed where software *ran* or how it was *sold*. This one changes who *builds* it. That is why this cycle is different in kind, and why the cycles are compressing: each successive shift takes less time to propagate. Five years is now the generous estimate, not the planning assumption.

## 3. Not death — the last product cycle

"Software is dying" confuses the container with the contents. Demand for software rises every single cycle; what churns is the packaging. Businesses will always have idiosyncratic workflows, and idiosyncratic workflows will always need someone to own intent, correctness, and accountability — generation is not maintenance, and maintenance is not fit.

But honesty requires the sharper version of the claim: **this is likely the last cycle in which the unit of value is a *product*. After it, the unit is the *outcome*** — software that increasingly configures itself per customer, priced per result rather than per seat. That is why there are "only a few years left of momentum, not necessarily of life." Momentum ends; franchises persist. Companies founded late in a window still lived for decades. We are not trying to build the last SaaS company. We are trying to build the best company of SaaS's last era.

## 4. Three camps

1. **The obituarists.** Pure software is dead; AI writes everything. We disagree — someone must own accountability and fit, and nobody pays for raw generation.
2. **The retreaters.** AI is permanently constrained, so pull back to deterministic, traditional SaaS with AI sprinkled on. This camp is *anti-AI* in instinct. We disagree with the prescription even though we credit the diagnosis (see §5). There is no going back: buyers have seen the demo. Anti-AI positioning will age exactly like anti-cloud positioning did.
3. **The natives (us).** AI is the starting place. The model is the substrate; the application is built around it — not the other way around.

## 5. The three drawbacks are real — and irrelevant as a defense

The retreaters correctly identify AI's three core weaknesses:

| Weakness | What customers actually need | Who is already fixing it |
|---|---|---|
| **Memory** | Insight — *it knows my world* | Memory harnesses and long-running-task runtimes |
| **UI** | Understanding — *I can see and steer what it did* | Claude artifacts, Cursor canvases, generative UI |
| **Repeatable actions** | Automation — *do it again, identically* | Skills, workflows, scheduled agents |

Here is the error: these three are treated as a moat against AI. They are not. A weakness that the strongest companies on earth are spending billions to erase is not a moat — it is a roadmap written for you. Supplementing AI with memory, surfaces, and repeatability is extremely easy, and it has already been done. **You cannot sell a patch for a hole the platform vendor is already pouring concrete into.** And even if you could: why would anyone buy a third subscription to fix the two they already pay for?

## 6. The one remaining differentiator: the generalist's dilemma

Strip away everything the giants will absorb, and exactly one thing remains — **the inherent generalism the big players cannot escape.** It is structural, not accidental:

- Training and inference economics favor the median user.
- Support, liability, and brand risk scale with specificity.
- A surface built for millions of contexts must settle at lowest-common-denominator UX — pushing the residual labor back onto the user as unpaid configuration. Prompting is the new setup wizard.
- Their roadmap optimizes for *useful to everyone*, which mathematically caps *perfect for you*.

The market lives in the gap between *capable* and *tailored*. Claude can do your job; it just doesn't know your job. Niches do not buy capability — they buy familiarity: vocabulary, edge cases, defaults, integrations, the sense that the vendor has lived in their world.

So the path is not "traditional SaaS plus cool AI features." It is: take what has already been built — **2025 was the year of the AI agents, 2026 is the year of the harness** — and push it up the stack into **niche specialization**, the same way value always moved up the stack once the layer beneath commoditized (chips → OS → apps; agents → harnesses → specialized applications). The spec for the product is one sentence: **as easy as Claude, tailored entirely to you.**

What it looks like in practice is unknowable from the armchair — it comes from iterating against real users. But the shape is known: hide the prompt, encode the niche's vocabulary and defaults, and own the workflow end to end.

## 7. On moats: no ace, a very good deck

If your mind says "there's no moat there," you're largely right — and it doesn't matter. Absence of a moat is not absence of a market. There is a **space in the shadow of the generalist giants** that needs filling, and filling a space is a business even when defending it isn't a fortress.

A moat is just an ace up your sleeve. But most hands are won by how the deck is played, not by one holding. Our deck:

- **Sales as co-product.** In a niche, distribution is the product's twin. Small worlds have fast reputations; case studies compound.
- **Trade secrets over patents.** Proprietary pipelines, curated corpora, entity graphs, evaluation rubrics — the accumulated judgment of having done the work. These don't demo well, which is precisely why they survive.
- **Earned moats over structural ones.** Data exhaust, workflow embeddedness, switching costs built through depth — none exist on day one; all compound if we outrun their decay.
- **Speed.** Assume every capability advantage expires quarterly, because the giants restock their releases quarterly.

Playing the game *means* compounding earned advantages faster than they decay.

## 8. The window dictates the posture

Because momentum has an end date:

- **Default alive.** Profitability inside ~36 months is the constraint that makes every other choice free. We are not buying growth with money we don't have to win a decade we won't need.
- **Quarterly falsification:** *if the generalists ship our category next quarter, what remains?* The answer must always be tailoring, data, and relationships — never a feature.
- **Optionality over empire.** A profitable company that filled its space can be scaled, merged, sold, or simply harvested. Not every good company needs to be a forever company.

## 9. What would prove us wrong

1. **Generalists go niche and stick it** — marketplace-grade vertical agents with real accountability. Watch: whether frontier vendors fund vertical-specific support, liability, and sales motions rather than just capabilities.
2. **The tailoring gap collapses** — self-configuring agents and near-free customization let customers become their own vendor. Watch: cost of production-quality fine-tuning/evals trending to zero for non-experts.
3. **Re-consolidation** — comfort pulls buyers back into one generalist suite for everything. Watch: net retention of horizontal suites vs. specialist churn.

If any of these fire before we're entrenched, the window closed early — and the correct response is harvest, not pivot.

## 10. Operating principles

1. The model is the substrate, never a feature.
2. Sell outcomes; price outcomes where possible.
3. Pick niches by pain × vocabulary-gap × generalist-neglect.
4. "As easy as Claude, specific to you" is the minimum viable magic.
5. Trade secrets over features; rubrics over demos.
6. Sales is not downstream of product — it is the co-product.
7. Compound earned advantages faster than they decay.
8. Default alive, inside the window, on purpose.
